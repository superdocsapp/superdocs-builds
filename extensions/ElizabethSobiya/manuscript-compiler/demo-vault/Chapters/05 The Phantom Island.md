# The Phantom Island

Sandy Island appeared in the South Pacific in 1876, stayed for one hundred and
thirty-six years, and was removed in 2012 by a research vessel that sailed through it.

![[Figures/sandy-island-1876.png|The 1876 chart entry, redrawn, showing the reported extent and the sounding that was never taken.]]

The island had been reported by a whaling ship, entered onto an Admiralty chart, and
copied outward from there into every general atlas that took its base from the
Admiralty. Tavares and colleagues traced eleven separate phantom entries of the same
period and found that nine originated in a single reporting chain [@tavares1996].

What is striking is not that the error was made. A tired lookout at dusk, a bank of
cloud, a shallow patch of discoloured water — the report was reasonable. What is
striking is the hundred and thirty-six years.

## The cost of removal

Nobody is paid to remove things from maps.

Adding a feature has a customer: someone wanted the road drawn. Removing one has no
customer, only a cost, and the cost falls on whoever is brave enough to assert a
negative.[^negative] The asymmetry is structural and it is why phantom features
outlive the people who report them.

[^negative]: The National Survey Office's own revision policy states that a feature is
    removed only on "positive evidence of absence" [@ordnance2019] — a standard that
    is far harder to meet than the standard for adding one.

Sandy Island survived because deleting it required a ship.
