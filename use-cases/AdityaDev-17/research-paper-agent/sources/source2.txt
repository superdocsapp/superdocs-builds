A central design decision in any retrieval-augmented generation system is how source documents are chunked before being indexed. Fixed-size chunking, which splits text into equal-length segments regardless of sentence or paragraph boundaries, is simple to implement but can split a single coherent idea across two chunks, weakening retrieval quality. Paragraph-aware or semantic chunking attempts to preserve natural boundaries, generally improving the coherence of what gets retrieved, at the cost of more complex preprocessing.

Retrieval quality is commonly measured along two axes: whether the correct passage was retrieved at all (recall), and whether the retrieved passage actually supports the claim the generator produces from it (grounding). A system can have excellent recall — consistently surfacing the right document — while still producing ungrounded output if the generation step doesn't faithfully restrict itself to what the retrieved passages actually say. This is why some RAG architectures add a separate verification pass: after a claim is generated alongside its supporting passage, an independent check re-reads the passage and confirms the claim is actually supported by it, catching cases where the generator overstated or misattributed something.

In legal question-answering specifically, this grounding requirement is especially strict, since a claim that sounds plausible but isn't actually stated in the cited case or statute can carry real consequences if relied upon. This has made verification-augmented RAG pipelines, which separate the drafting and fact-checking steps into distinct stages, an increasingly common pattern for high-stakes domains.
